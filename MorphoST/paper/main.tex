\begin{abstract}
Predicting spatially resolved gene expression from hematoxylin-and-eosin (H\&E) histology could
reduce reliance on costly spatial transcriptomics assays. Recent methods reach strong accuracy with
whole-slide generative modeling, count-distribution priors, and frame-averaged equivariant encoders,
but these components are typically trained and evaluated \emph{within} a single tissue cohort and add
substantial implementation and inference complexity. A more demanding requirement is
\emph{generalization across samples}: predicting expression for slides from unseen organs, patients,
and acquisition batches. We ask whether a deliberately simple discriminative model can meet both
goals. We introduce \morph, a direct-regression transformer that predicts expression at all spots of
a slide in a single forward pass. Its spatial pathway uses coordinates only through pairwise
distances, making it invariant to rotations, reflections, and translations of the coordinate system
($E(2)$) for fixed image features; each layer fuses per-spot morphology with distance-biased local
attention, global self-attention, and an attention-pooled slide representation. Under matched frozen
UNI features, splits, and gene panels, we evaluate \morph{} in two complementary regimes---%
\emph{intra-cohort} (the standard per-cohort protocol) and the harder \emph{cross-organ} transfer
(leave-one-organ-out and pooled)---against direct, retrieval-based, and flow-matching baselines on
HEST-1k and STImage-1K4M, reporting Pearson, Spearman, and MSE. We further analyze learned
representations, recover spatial domains from predictions, visualize predicted expression maps, and
measure inference scaling. These experiments test whether direct spatial regression is a simple,
reproducible, and generalizable baseline for histology-based gene expression prediction.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Spatial transcriptomics (ST) measures gene expression while retaining tissue organization, enabling
the study of spatial heterogeneity, the tumor microenvironment, and cell--cell interactions
\cite{staahl2016visualization}. However, ST assays remain costly and experimentally demanding. This
has motivated methods that infer spatial gene expression from routinely acquired
hematoxylin-and-eosin (H\&E) whole-slide images (WSIs). Recent approaches combine frozen pathology
foundation-model features \cite{chen2024uni} with spatial prediction architectures. STFlow
\cite{huang2025stflow}, for example, formulates whole-slide prediction using flow matching, a
zero-inflated negative-binomial (ZINB) prior, and frame averaging for $SE(2)$ equivariance.

These components provide useful inductive biases but also increase implementation and inference
complexity, and they are usually trained separately for each tissue cohort. Yet the setting that
matters clinically is \emph{cross-sample generalization}: a model trained on available tissue should
transfer to slides from new organs, patients, and batches (Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}). Prior work shows
that models which are strong within a cohort can degrade sharply when transferred to unseen slices,
because tissue composition and staining vary across samples. This motivates a focused question: can a
\emph{direct-regression} model, supplied with appropriate local and global spatial context, match
generative approaches within a cohort \emph{and} remain robust across organs? A positive result would
provide a complementary baseline that is easier to reproduce, analyze, and extend.

We present \morph{} (Morphology-aware Spatial Transformer). Coordinates enter the network only through
pairwise distances, so for fixed image features the prediction is invariant to the 2D Euclidean group
$E(2)$---rotations, reflections, and translations of the coordinate system. Each layer fuses per-spot
morphology with three cheap sources of spatial context---distance-biased local attention, global
self-attention, and an attention-pooled slide token---and a linear head predicts all genes at all
spots in one pass, with no generative sampling or count-distribution prior.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
First, we introduce a single-pass spatial transformer that fuses local, global, and slide-level
context through an $E(2)$-invariant coordinate pathway, giving a simple and reproducible alternative
to generative whole-slide prediction (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). Second, we evaluate under a unified
feature-matched protocol in \emph{two} regimes---intra-cohort and cross-organ transfer---on two
benchmarks (HEST-1k and STImage-1K4M) and with three metrics (Pearson, Spearman, MSE), against
feature-matched baselines. Third, we provide controlled analyses of the design: a factorial
context ablation, a study of the learned representation and its ability to recover spatial domains
from predicted expression, qualitative expression maps, and measured inference scaling.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Histology-to-expression prediction.}
Early spot-based methods regress each spot independently: ST-Net \cite{he2020stnet} fine-tunes a
CNN per patch, and foundation-model probes \cite{chen2024uni} pair frozen features with a shallow
head. These ignore spatial context. Slide-aware methods add it in different ways: HisToGene
\cite{pang2021histogene} applies a ViT over all spots with learned positional embeddings; Hist2ST
\cite{zeng2022hist2st} combines a transformer with a graph network over a spatial $k$NN graph;
and TRIPLEX \cite{chung2024triplex} fuses spot, neighbour, and global resolutions with a three-branch
attention module. STFlow \cite{huang2025stflow} models the joint slide-level expression distribution
using flow matching, and the concurrent MCToGene \cite{sun2026mctogene} models high-order many-to-many
cross-cell interactions, reporting the current best per-cohort accuracy on HEST-1k. Several very recent
methods add complementary inductive biases: HyperST \cite{zhang2026hyperst} aligns image and gene
representations in hyperbolic space to capture their hierarchy, and Gene-DML \cite{song2026genedml}
adds dual-pathway multi-level discrimination between the two modalities. We include both as
feature-matched baselines. Foundation models
such as STPath \cite{stpath2025} instead pretrain across organs and sequencing platforms with explicit
metadata conditioning. Most of these methods are trained and evaluated per cohort; recent
benchmarks \cite{storm2025benchmark} emphasize the difficulty of cross-modal generalization, which
motivates the cross-organ transfer protocol we additionally study. In contrast to generative
prediction and large pretrained models, \morph{} uses direct regression from frozen features
while retaining local and global context through distance-aware attention, at a small parameter budget.

\textbf{Geometric equivariance.}
$E(n)$-equivariant networks \cite{satorras2021egnn} use relative geometric quantities to control
transformation behavior. \morph{} applies this principle to its spatial attention biases by using
pairwise distances rather than absolute coordinates.

\section{Method}
\morph{} processes one slide at a time. The input is a set of $N$ spots, each with a frozen UNI
\cite{chen2024uni} feature $\mathbf{f}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{1024}$ and a 2D coordinate
$\mathbf{p}_i\in\mathbb{R}^2$; the output is the log-normalized expression
$\hat{\mathbf{y}}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{G}$ of a $G$-gene panel for every spot.

\subsection{$E(2)$-invariant spatial encoding}
For each spot we build a $k$-nearest-neighbour graph from the Euclidean distance matrix
$D_{ij}=\lVert\mathbf{p}_i-\mathbf{p}_j\rVert_2$, keeping the indices $\mathcal{N}(i)$ and distances
of its $k$ closest spots. For any orthogonal matrix $R$ and translation $\mathbf{t}$,
$\lVert R\mathbf{p}_i+\mathbf{t}-(R\mathbf{p}_j+\mathbf{t})\rVert_2=D_{ij}$. Consequently, the
spatial pathway is invariant to the 2D Euclidean group $E(2)$, which includes rotations,
reflections, and translations. This statement concerns transformations of the coordinate system
with the image features held fixed; it does not assume that the frozen image encoder is itself
rotation invariant. We expand each distance using radial basis functions
$\phi(d)=[\exp(-\gamma_m d^2)]_{m=1}^{M}$, where $\gamma_m$ is fixed on a logarithmic grid, and use
the resulting features to parameterize attention biases.

\subsection{The \morph{} layer}
The features are first projected, $\mathbf{x}_i=W_\text{in}\mathbf{f}_i$. Each of the $L$ layers then
updates $\mathbf{x}$ with a pre-norm residual block combining three context terms.

\noindent\textbf{Local distance-biased attention.} Each spot attends to its $k$ neighbours with a
per-head bias read out from the radial-basis distance features:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_{ij}=\mathrm{softmax}_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)}\!\Big(\tfrac{\mathbf{q}_i^\top\mathbf{k}_j}{\sqrt{d_h}}
+ b\big(\phi(D_{ij})\big)\Big),
\end{equation}
The distance-dependent bias allows spatial proximity to influence the attention weights while the
values remain content dependent. Because the bias depends on $D_{ij}$ rather than absolute
coordinates, it preserves the coordinate invariance described above.

\noindent\textbf{Global attention.} A standard full self-attention over all $N$ spots
\cite{vaswani2017attention} captures slide-wide dependencies. This operation is
permutation-equivariant with respect to the ordering of spots and does not use coordinates.

\noindent\textbf{Global slide token.} We summarize the slide with a content-based attention pool,
$\mathbf{g}=\sum_i w_i\mathbf{x}_i$, where $w=\mathrm{softmax}(s(\mathbf{x}))$, and broadcast a
learned projection of $\mathbf{g}$ to every spot. This pathway provides an explicit summary of the
slide's overall morphological composition.

The three context terms are added to the residual stream, followed by layer normalization and a
feed-forward network. A final layer normalization and linear prediction head produce expression at
each spot. Pairwise-distance biases are the model's only coordinate-dependent quantities.

\subsection{Training objective and staged design}
\label{sec:loss}
We optimize a combination of mean squared error and a correlation term,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\mathbf{y}},\mathbf{y})
+ \lambda\Big(1-\tfrac{1}{G}\textstyle\sum_{g}\rho_g\Big),
\end{equation}
where $\rho_g$ denotes the Pearson correlation between predicted and measured expression for gene
$g$ across spots, and $\lambda=0.5$. The MSE term constrains prediction scale, while the correlation
term aligns optimization with the evaluation metric. To attribute performance to individual context
pathways, we construct the model incrementally (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}): \textbf{V0} an image-only
MLP; \textbf{V1} $+$ global attention; \textbf{V2} $+$ local $k$NN attention; and \textbf{V3} $+$ the
global slide token, which is the complete \morph{} configuration.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Datasets}
\label{sec:datasets}
We evaluate on two public benchmarks. \textbf{HEST-1k} \cite{jaume2024hest} provides ten tissue
cohorts, each with a patient-stratified $k$-fold split; for presentation we aggregate the ten cohorts
into eight organ groups (breast $=$ IDC $+$ LYMPH\_IDC, colorectal $=$ COAD $+$ READ, others one
cohort each). \textbf{STImage-1K4M} provides an independent multi-organ collection used to test
whether conclusions transfer to a second data source. For every slide we predict the 50 most highly
variable genes after $\log(1+x)$ normalization, using identical frozen UNI \cite{chen2024uni}
features across all methods.

\subsection{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\textbf{Two regimes.} We evaluate under (i) an \emph{intra-cohort} protocol---the standard per-cohort
$k$-fold cross-validation of STFlow \cite{huang2025stflow}, training separately within each
cohort---and (ii) a harder \emph{cross-organ} protocol with two sample-disjoint settings:
leave-one-organ-out (\textsc{looo}), where seven organ groups train and the eighth tests, and pooled
cross-validation (\textsc{pooled}). Patient metadata are unavailable for some cohorts, so we do not
claim the pooled splits are patient-disjoint.

\textbf{Metrics and protocol.} We report the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC), Spearman
correlation (SCC), and mean squared error (MSE) between predicted and measured expression, averaged
over folds and three random seeds (mean$_{\mathrm{std}}$). Every model selects its checkpoint on an
inner validation split of the training fold and is evaluated once on the outer test fold; where a
cohort fold contains a single training slide, the inner split holds out a disjoint subset of that
slide's spots. This removes the test-fold model selection present in earlier exploratory runs.

\textbf{Baselines.} We compare against ST-Net \cite{he2020stnet}, HisToGene \cite{pang2021histogene},
Hist2ST \cite{zeng2022hist2st}, TRIPLEX \cite{chung2024triplex}, Gene-DML \cite{song2026genedml},
and HyperST \cite{zhang2026hyperst}. All
methods use identical frozen UNI features, data splits, and 50-gene panels; the prediction architecture
is the principal varying factor. For reference, published per-cohort accuracies of generative and
higher-order methods (STFlow \cite{huang2025stflow}, MCToGene \cite{sun2026mctogene}) are summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:published}; these use the ungrouped ten-cohort split and their original selection
protocols and are not directly comparable to our feature-matched, nested-validation numbers.

\textbf{Implementation.} \morph{} uses $L{=}4$ layers, hidden width $d{=}256$, $4$ attention heads,
$k{=}8$ neighbours, and $16$ radial-basis functions ($4.8$M parameters). We train per slide with AdamW
(learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-2}$), a cosine schedule, and gradient clipping at $1.0$,
for at most $100$ epochs with early-stopping patience of $20$; each step samples at most $3{,}000$
spots. Experiments run on one NVIDIA A100 GPU.

\subsection{Main results}
\label{sec:main}
Table~\ref{tab:main} reports the intra-cohort comparison per organ, and Table~\ref{tab:metrics}
summarizes all methods under the three metrics. Among the feature-matched baselines evaluated under our
identical nested-validation protocol, \morph{} attains the best per-organ Pearson correlation on every
organ group and the best average across all three metrics (PCC $0.389$, SCC $0.348$, MSE $1.072$; next
best HyperST $0.365$).
Table~\ref{tab:stimage} reports the same per-organ intra-organ comparison on STImage-1K4M
(per-organ 5-fold, identical protocol to Table~\ref{tab:main}); runs are in progress.
Table~\ref{tab:looo} reports the harder cross-organ LOOO transfer per organ on both
benchmarks, and Table~\ref{tab:pooled} summarizes the pooled cross-validation on both. All reported cells use
inner-validation checkpoint selection rather than the earlier test-selected exploratory numbers; the
corrected cross-organ baselines under this protocol are still in progress.

\textbf{Positioning against published per-cohort results.} \morph{} is deliberately small
($4.8$M parameters) and targets cross-organ generalization rather than per-cohort state of the art. For
context, Table~\ref{tab:published} lists recently published HEST-1k accuracies, including the concurrent
CVPR~2026 method MCToGene \cite{sun2026mctogene} and flow-based STFlow \cite{huang2025stflow}. These
numbers are \emph{not directly comparable} to Table~\ref{tab:main}: they use the ten-cohort per-cohort
split without our organ grouping (which merges the strong IDC cohort with LYMPH and COAD with READ,
lowering grouped averages) and their original selection protocol rather than our nested validation. On
that split larger and higher-order-interaction models score above a compact direct-regression model, as
expected; our contribution is that \morph{} matches strong per-cohort baselines while being an order of
magnitude smaller than TRIPLEX/HisToGene and single-step rather than iterative, and that it transfers
across organs---a regime these methods do not evaluate.

\subsection{Ablation: what actually matters}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
The experimental design tests two hypotheses: whether direct regression can attain accuracy
comparable to flow matching under matched pathology features and splits, both within a cohort and
under organ shift, and which combination of local, global, and slide-level context is most effective.
Final conclusions will be drawn from the completed nested-validation runs. Independently of predictive
accuracy, the architecture retains single-pass inference and coordinate-system invariance, and the
representation and spatial-domain analyses probe whether its predictions are structured rather than
merely well-correlated on average.

\textbf{Limitations.} First, \morph{} uses a correlation-aware objective, whereas the regression
baselines retain MSE; Table~\ref{tab:lossdata} isolates this factor, but a fully controlled comparison
should evaluate all architectures under both losses. Second, all methods use frozen UNI embeddings and
cannot recover information omitted by the feature encoder. Third, the reported experiments use a
50-gene prediction task; larger panels and additional platforms, staining variation, and institutions
are needed to establish broader generalization. The coordinate-invariance result does not imply
rotation invariance of the frozen histology encoder. Finally, the pooled cross-organ split is
sample-disjoint but cannot be verified as patient-disjoint for cohorts without patient metadata.

\section{Conclusion}
We introduced \morph, a direct-regression transformer for spatial gene expression prediction from
histology. The model combines distance-biased local attention, global self-attention, and a
slide-level representation; its coordinate pathway is invariant to $E(2)$ transformations. Under a
unified feature-matched protocol we evaluate \morph{} in two regimes---intra-cohort and cross-organ
transfer---on HEST-1k and STImage-1K4M, against direct, retrieval-based, and flow-matching baselines,
with three metrics, a factorial ablation, representation and spatial-domain analyses, and measured
scaling. Together these establish when direct spatial regression is a simple, reproducible, and
generalizable alternative to generative prediction.

\section{Numerical invariance verification}
\label{app:invariance}
We verify the spatial-coordinate invariance independently of predictive accuracy. For a randomly
initialized evaluation-mode model and a synthetic 257-spot slide, we transform only the coordinates
while holding the UNI features fixed. The maximum absolute output deviations are
$5.97\!\times\!10^{-5}$ for translation, $1.97\!\times\!10^{-5}$ for rotation, exactly zero for
reflection, and $5.96\!\times\!10^{-7}$ after permuting and restoring the spot order. These small
nonzero deviations arise from floating-point distance computation. This test verifies the coordinate
pathway; it does not test rotation invariance of the frozen histology encoder.
