\title{\morph: Multi-Scale Coordinate-Invariant Spatial Attention\\
for Gene Expression Prediction from Histology}

\author{Anonymous}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Predicting spatial gene expression from H\&E histology images could expand access to
spatial molecular analysis, but requires models to capture tissue organization across multiple
spatial scales. Existing approaches either ignore spatial coordinates entirely, encode absolute
positions that break under coordinate-frame changes, or use binary $k$NN adjacency that discards
actual inter-spot distances. No prior method encodes continuous pairwise distances while remaining
invariant to coordinate transformations.
We introduce \morph{}, a compact spatial transformer that combines three complementary signals in
each layer: $k$NN attention biased by \emph{continuous} pairwise distances for local tissue
neighborhoods, global self-attention for dependencies across the slide, and attention pooling that
forms a slide representation and broadcasts morphological context to every spot. Because coordinates
enter the spatial pathway only through pairwise Euclidean distances encoded as radial-basis features,
the model is invariant to coordinate-frame changes \emph{and} retains richer spatial information than
binary-adjacency models, which treat all neighbors as equally close.
We evaluate \morph{} on seven human organ cohorts from HEST-1k under a controlled protocol in which
all methods use identical frozen UNI+CONCH embeddings, gene panels, data splits, and validation
procedures. When trained and evaluated within each cohort, \morph{} achieves a mean Pearson correlation of $0.470$,
improving over the strongest baseline at $0.448$ by $4.9\%$ and leading on six of seven cohorts.
Under the more challenging leave one organ out (LOOO) setting, it reaches $0.490$, remaining competitive
with the best matched baseline at $0.500$. Factorial ablations identify local attention biased by distance as
the primary contributor on the evaluated cohorts. With $4.88$M parameters and
$64.7$\,ms inference for a slide with 10,000 spots on an A100, \morph{} provides an efficient and
geometrically principled model for spatial gene expression prediction from histology.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Spatial transcriptomics (ST) measures gene expression together with tissue location, enabling the
study of spatial heterogeneity, tissue organization, and cellular interactions
\cite{staahl2016visualization}. Its cost and experimental burden, however, constrain the number and
diversity of specimens that can be profiled. In contrast, H\&E slides are routinely collected and
contain morphological signals associated with tissue composition and molecular state. Predicting
spatial gene expression from these images could therefore extend molecular analysis to specimens
without matched ST measurements.

The central difficulty is that morphology and expression are related at more than one level. A
spot contains local visual evidence, nearby spots define its tissue neighborhood, distant regions
can share structure, and the composition of the slide can shift the expected expression profile.
Existing methods capture parts of this context through independent patch regression
\cite{he2020stnet}, spatial graphs and transformers
\cite{pang2021histogene,zeng2022hist2st}, cross-modal retrieval \cite{xie2023bleep}, or conditional
generation \cite{zhu2025diffusion}. Yet adding spatial context introduces a second concern: a model
that consumes absolute positions can depend on the scanner coordinate frame. The same relative
tissue arrangement may then produce different predictions after its coordinates are translated,
rotated, or reflected.

This tension raises a natural question: \emph{can a compact predictor combine neighborhood context
and context across the slide without becoming sensitive to an arbitrary coordinate frame?} We address this
question by reconsidering two common design choices.

\noindent\textbf{Coordinate representation.}
Spatial models broadly fall into three groups. Methods such as Hist2ST~\cite{zeng2022hist2st} and
HisToGene~\cite{pang2021histogene} embed absolute $x,y$ integer positions, making predictions
sensitive to the scanner coordinate frame (rotation drops mean PCC from 1.00 to $\approx$0.5 in our
experiments). Methods such as TRIPLEX~\cite{chung2024triplex}, HyperST~\cite{zhang2026hyperst} and
M2OST~\cite{wang2025m2ost} instead build a binary $k$NN adjacency matrix from spot coordinates and
aggregate neighbor features with uniform weights; because pairwise distances are rotation-invariant,
these models are coordinate-frame invariant, but the adjacency discards the actual inter-spot
distances, so all neighbors within the graph are treated as equally close regardless of how near or
far they actually are. \morph{} takes a third path: it encodes continuous pairwise distances as
radial-basis features that modulate per-head attention weights, preserving full distance information
while remaining invariant to coordinate-frame changes by construction.

\noindent\textbf{Context representation.}
Local aggregation, interactions across the slide, and a summary of slide composition play distinct
roles. Representing them as separate pathways lets a model combine these signals while making their
individual contributions directly testable through factorial ablation.

\insightbox{What We Do.}{We represent every measured spot as a token and let it learn from three
sources in parallel: nearby spots, all spots on the slide, and a summary of the slide. Coordinates
are used only to measure distances between spots. Consequently, changing the origin or orientation
of the coordinate system does not change the prediction when the image features remain fixed.}

Building on these observations, we introduce \morph{}, a compact spatial transformer for direct gene
expression prediction (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). Each layer combines three pathways: $k$NN attention
biased by pairwise distance aggregates local neighborhoods, full self-attention models dependencies
across the slide, and attention pooling constructs a slide representation that is broadcast to every
spot. The three outputs are fused residually before the expression head. For fixed histology
features, \morph{} is invariant to translations, rotations, and reflections of the coordinate frame
because coordinates affect the network only through pairwise distances. This guarantee concerns
transformations of the coordinate representation; it does not assume that the frozen image encoder
is invariant to transformations of the histology image itself.

We evaluate \morph{} on seven human organ cohorts from HEST-1k using matched image features, gene
panels, data splits, optimization budgets, and nested validation. We distinguish prediction within
individual cohorts, pooled training across represented organs, and leave one organ out (LOOO)
transfer to an anatomy absent from training. Within cohorts, \morph{} achieves a mean Pearson
correlation of $0.470$, compared with $0.448$ for the strongest baseline, and leads on six of seven
cohorts. It reaches $0.760$ in pooled evaluation and $0.490$ under LOOO transfer. In the expanded
matched LOOO comparison, EGN obtains the highest average at $0.500$. A factorial ablation on SKCM
and LUNG identifies local attention as the dominant contributor, while the complete model achieves
the highest mean. \morph{} uses $4.88$M parameters and predicts a slide with 10,000 spots in
$64.7$\,ms on an A100.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
Our contributions are threefold:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item We introduce \morph{}, a multi-scale spatial transformer that fuses local, slide-wide, and
  slide-summary context in every layer using \emph{continuous} pairwise-distance RBF features as the
  sole coordinate signal. This is the first ST prediction model to use continuous inter-spot distances
  rather than binary adjacency, making it both coordinate-frame invariant and distance-expressive: the
  attention bias shifts continuously with proximity, unlike uniform neighbor aggregation in TRIPLEX,
  HyperST, and M2OST.
  \item We conduct a rotation-sensitivity experiment across all seven baselines on real Visium data,
  showing that absolute-position models (Hist2ST: PCC $=0.51$) are strongly frame-dependent, while
  both binary-adjacency and \morph{} achieve PCC $\geq0.997$, and clarifying that the distinction
  between them lies in distance expressiveness rather than invariance alone.
  \item We provide a controlled HEST-1k evaluation across within-cohort, pooled, and unseen-organ
  settings, together with factorial context ablations and measurements of computational efficiency.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\noindent\textbf{Machine Learning Prediction of Gene Expression from Histology.}\quad
ST-Net \cite{he2020stnet} established patch-level regression from histology to expression.
HisToGene \cite{pang2021histogene} and Hist2ST \cite{zeng2022hist2st} subsequently introduced
slide-level attention and spatial graphs, while TRIPLEX \cite{chung2024triplex} combines local,
neighbourhood, and global morphology. BLEEP \cite{xie2023bleep} follows a different strategy: it
aligns image and expression embeddings and retrieves expression from similar training examples.
More recent models incorporate higher-order cellular interactions \cite{sun2026mctogene}, dual-path
discrimination \cite{song2026genedml}, or hyperbolic cross-modal structure
\cite{zhang2026hyperst}. M2OST \cite{wang2025m2ost} integrates multiscale image regions for
many-to-one regression, whereas EGN \cite{yang2023egn} augments a patch predictor with retrieved
exemplars. Because their original evaluations vary in features, gene panels, splits,
and model-selection procedures, published scores do not by themselves isolate architectural gains.

STEM \cite{zhu2025diffusion} frames expression inference as conditional generation rather than
deterministic regression. This distinction is important: a generator can represent multiple
expression profiles compatible with similar morphology, whereas \morph{} estimates one profile per
spot. Our objective is therefore narrower. We ask whether an inexpensive point predictor can provide
a strong accuracy and transfer baseline under a matched protocol; we do not claim to model biological
uncertainty or the full conditional expression distribution.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Geometric Structure and Coordinate Invariance.}\quad
Spatial prediction depends on the coordinate representation supplied to a model. Absolute position
embeddings can encode location within a particular coordinate frame, but their output may change
when an equivalent tissue layout is translated, rotated, or reflected. Geometric networks address
this issue by constructing interactions from relative quantities \cite{satorras2021egnn}. \morph{}
uses an invariant construction in which coordinates enter its spatial pathway only through pairwise
Euclidean distances. Because these distances are preserved by translations, rotations, and
reflections, no transformed coordinate frames or prediction averaging are required. This guarantee
applies to the spatial pathway for fixed histology features; it does not imply that the frozen image
encoder is invariant to rotations of the image itself.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Generalization across Samples and Organs.}\quad
HEST-1k \cite{jaume2024hest} spans many cohorts and organs, yet its standard predictive benchmark is
organized within cohorts. A model can therefore exploit organ-specific morphology, expression
statistics, or acquisition effects. Cross-sample benchmarking also identifies distribution shift as
a central challenge \cite{storm2025benchmark}. We separate two forms of multi-organ evaluation:
pooled cross-validation holds out samples while retaining their organ types in training, whereas
LOOO withholds an entire organ. The latter is a stricter test of anatomical transfer.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Positioning of \morph{}.}
\morph{} connects these three research directions: it is an inductive-bias-explicit spatial
transformer for direct regression, evaluated under a matched cross-cohort protocol. Unlike
absolute position encodings or frame-averaging strategies, its coordinate pathway is invariant by
construction. Unlike generative models, it does not represent expression uncertainty; its advantage
is deterministic, single-pass inference with explicit geometric structure. Unlike prior evaluations
that report within-cohort accuracy only, our controlled comparison separates interpolation from
anatomical transfer, making the difficulty of organ shift directly measurable.

\section{Method}

\noindent\textbf{Problem Set-up.}\quad
For a slide with $N$ spots, let
$\mathbf{F}=[\mathbf{f}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{f}_N]^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$
denote frozen pathology features and
$\mathbf{P}=[\mathbf{p}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{p}_N]^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times2}$ the corresponding
spot coordinates. The target
$\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times G}$ contains log-normalized expression for $G$ genes. We learn
$f_\theta:(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{P})\mapsto\hat{\mathbf{Y}}$ and process each slide independently;
the model therefore supports a variable number of spots and requires no expression measurements at
test time. In the feature-matched main comparisons, $\mathbf{f}_i$ concatenates frozen UNI
\cite{chen2024uni} and CONCH \cite{lu2024conch} embeddings ($d_{\mathrm{in}}=1536$).

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Continuous Distance Encoding.}\quad
We first compute the pairwise Euclidean distance matrix
$D_{ij}=\lVert\mathbf{p}_i-\mathbf{p}_j\rVert_2$. For each spot $i$, we retain the indices
$\mathcal{N}(i)$ and distances of its $k$ nearest neighbours, excluding the spot itself. Distances
are expanded into $M$ radial-basis features,
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\phi}(D_{ij})=
\left[\exp\!\left(-\gamma_m D_{ij}^{2}\right)\right]_{m=1}^{M},
\end{equation}
where the fixed bandwidth parameters $\gamma_m$ are logarithmically spaced. These features influence
attention weights but are never added to the spot embeddings as absolute positions.

\begin{proposition}[Coordinate frame invariance]
\label{prop:coordinate_invariance}
Let $R\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times2}$ be orthogonal, let $\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$, and define
$\mathbf{P}'=\mathbf{P}R^\top+\mathbf{1}\mathbf{t}^\top$. For fixed histology features
$\mathbf{F}$, the prediction is unchanged:
\begin{equation}
f_\theta(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{P}')=f_\theta(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{P}).
\end{equation}
Thus, the spatial pathway is invariant to translations, rotations, and reflections of the coordinate
frame in exact arithmetic.
\end{proposition}
The guarantee concerns a change of coordinate frame with fixed histology features; it does not claim
that the frozen image encoder is invariant to transformations of the histology image. The proof is in
Appendix~\ref{app:formal_guarantees}.
Note that binary $k$NN adjacency models (TRIPLEX, HyperST, M2OST) satisfy the same invariance
property, since pairwise distances are also rotation-invariant. The distinguishing property of
\morph{} is that it retains the \emph{magnitude} of those distances as continuous RBF features
influencing attention weights, whereas binary-adjacency models collapse each distance to a 0/1
neighbor indicator, losing the information of how close or far each neighbor actually is.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{The \morph{} Layer.}\quad
\insightbox{Core Idea.}{At every layer, each spot receives three messages in parallel: one from its
nearby spots, one from all spots, and one from a pooled summary of the slide. The model adds these
messages to the current spot representation and refines the result with a feed-forward network.
Only the nearby-spot message uses coordinates, and it uses pairwise distances rather than absolute
positions.}

The input features are projected to hidden width $d$,
$\mathbf{X}^{(0)}=\mathbf{F}W_{\mathrm{in}}+\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{in}}$. The same neighbour graph and
radial-basis features are reused in all $L$ transformer layers. At layer $\ell$, a pre-normalized
representation $\mathbf{H}=\operatorname{LN}(\mathbf{X}^{(\ell)})$ is passed through three context
pathways.

\noindent\textbf{Local distance-biased attention.} Each spot attends to its $k$ neighbours with a
learned, distance-dependent bias for each attention head. For head $h$, the attention weights are
\begin{equation}
\alpha_{ij}^{h}=\operatorname{softmax}_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)}\!\left(
\frac{(\mathbf{q}_i^{h})^\top\mathbf{k}_j^{h}}{\sqrt{d_h}}
+b_h\!\left(\boldsymbol{\phi}(D_{ij})\right)\right),
\end{equation}
where $d_h=d/H$ for $H$ heads. The local output is the concatenation of the weighted value sums,
followed by a learned output projection. Morphological similarity is captured by the query--key
term, while spatial distance contributes through the learned radial-basis bias.

\noindent\textbf{Global self-attention.} In parallel, standard multi-head self-attention
\cite{vaswani2017attention} allows every spot to attend to all $N$ spots. This pathway captures
long-range dependencies that may not be connected in the local graph. It uses morphology features
only and is permutation-equivariant with respect to spot order.

\noindent\textbf{Attention-pooled slide context.} A learned scalar scoring function produces
$a_i=\operatorname{softmax}_{i}(s(\mathbf{h}_i))$. The slide representation is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{g}=W_g\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_i\mathbf{h}_i\right)+\mathbf{b}_g,
\end{equation}
and is broadcast to every spot. Unlike a learned class token, this representation is computed
directly from the slide's spot features and summarizes its morphological composition.

For the complete V3 model, the local output, global output, and broadcast slide representation are
summed and added to the residual stream. A second pre-normalization residual update applies a
two-layer feed-forward network with expansion factor four and GELU activation:
\begin{align}
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{(\ell)}
  &=\mathbf{X}^{(\ell)}+\mathbf{L}^{(\ell)}+\mathbf{A}^{(\ell)}+\mathbf{G}^{(\ell)},\\
\mathbf{X}^{(\ell+1)}
  &=\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{(\ell)}+
    \operatorname{FFN}\!\left(\operatorname{LN}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{(\ell)})\right).
\end{align}
After $L$ layers, layer normalization and a linear head map each spot representation to its $G$
predicted gene values. The model produces the complete slide prediction in one forward pass.

\begin{proposition}[Permutation equivariance]
\label{prop:permutation_equivariance}
Let $Q\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}$ be a permutation matrix. If the $k$NN construction handles distance
ties equivariantly, then
\begin{equation}
f_\theta(Q\mathbf{F},Q\mathbf{P})=Qf_\theta(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{P}).
\end{equation}
Therefore, reordering the input spots only reorders their predicted expression profiles.
\end{proposition}
Appendix~\ref{app:formal_guarantees} provides the proof. Our numerical checks evaluate both
Propositions~\ref{prop:coordinate_invariance} and~\ref{prop:permutation_equivariance} under finite
precision.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Training Objective.}
\label{sec:loss}
For a slide with measured expression $\mathbf{Y}$, we optimize mean squared error together with a
gene-wise Pearson-correlation term:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\mathbf{Y}},\mathbf{Y})
+ \lambda\left(1-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{V}}\rho_g\right),
\end{equation}
where $\rho_g$ is the Pearson correlation between predicted and measured expression across the $N$
spots, $\mathcal{V}$ is the set of genes with nonzero target variance on that slide, and
$\lambda=0.5$. MSE constrains the scale of the predictions, while the correlation term encourages
the model to reproduce spot-to-spot expression patterns.

The main model enables all three context pathways. Our factorial ablation independently toggles the
local (L), global (G), and slide-level (S) pathways, avoiding conclusions tied to a particular order
of architectural additions (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).

\section{Experimental Results}

\noindent\textbf{Dataset and Preprocessing.}\quad
\label{sec:datasets}
We evaluate on \textbf{HEST-1k} \cite{jaume2024hest}. The controlled human-tissue benchmark retains
seven cohorts representing seven organs; HCC, LYMPH\_IDC, and READ are excluded for the data-quality
and split constraints documented in Appendix~\ref{app:cohort_stats}. Each cohort uses the provided
patient-stratified folds. We predict the 50 most \emph{highly variable genes} (\textbf{HVG}s)---genes ranked by
cross-spot expression variance---after $\log(1+x)$ normalization, using the same frozen
UNI+CONCH features for every method in the main comparison.
HVGs are the HEST-1k default panel and capture broad tissue-level expression variation.
For completeness, two alternative panel strategies appear in prior work:
\emph{differentially expressed genes} (\textbf{DEG}s), selected via unsupervised Leiden
clustering followed by Wilcoxon rank-sum tests across clusters, and \emph{histology-morphology
HVGs} (\textbf{HMHVG}s), which select HVGs by maximum Pearson correlation with PCA components
of the UNI+CONCH image features.
A controlled comparison of all three panels across baselines and \morph{} is reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:panel}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Experiment Setup.}\quad
\label{sec:setup}
\textbf{Evaluation regimes.} Intra-cohort evaluation trains a separate model within each cohort.
Pooled five-fold cross-validation trains jointly across organs and holds out samples from represented
organs. LOOO trains on six organs and tests the seventh, which is absent from training. Pooled folds
are sample-disjoint; because patient identifiers are unavailable for some cohorts, we do not claim
that they are patient-disjoint.

\textbf{Metrics and protocol.} We report the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC), Spearman
correlation (SCC), and mean squared error (MSE) between predicted and measured expression, averaged
over folds and three random seeds (mean$_{\mathrm{std}}$). Every model selects its checkpoint on an
inner-validation split of the training fold and is evaluated once on the outer test-fold; where a
cohort fold contains a single training slide, the inner split holds out a disjoint subset of that
slide's spots. This removes the test-fold model selection present in earlier exploratory runs.

\textbf{Baselines.} We compare with ST-Net \cite{he2020stnet}, Hist2ST
\cite{zeng2022hist2st}, HyperST \cite{zhang2026hyperst}, BLEEP \cite{xie2023bleep}, and STEM
\cite{zhu2025diffusion} where results are available. Features, gene panels, splits, optimization
budgets, and checkpoint selection are matched in the controlled tables. Published MCToGene results
\cite{sun2026mctogene}, which use a different ten-cohort protocol, appear separately in
Appendix~\ref{app:published} and are not treated as directly comparable.

\textbf{Implementation.} \morph{} uses $L{=}4$ layers, hidden width $d{=}256$, $4$ attention heads,
$k{=}8$ neighbours, and $16$ radial-basis functions ($4.8$M parameters). We train per slide with AdamW
(learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-2}$), a cosine schedule, and gradient clipping at $1.0$,
for at most $100$ epochs with early-stopping patience of $20$; each step samples at most $3{,}000$
spots. Experiments run on one NVIDIA A100 GPU.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{RQ1: Does \morph{} improve intra-cohort prediction?}\quad
\label{sec:rq1}
Table~\ref{tab:main} reports the feature-matched comparison. \morph{} obtains the highest mean PCC
($0.470$), compared with $0.448$ for HyperST and $0.426$ for BLEEP, a $4.9\%$ relative improvement
over the strongest baseline. It leads on six of seven cohorts; HyperST is higher on CCRCC by
$0.004$. The gain is therefore broad but not uniform, and it is largest on skin ($+0.054$ vs.\
HyperST) and prostate ($+0.026$).

\noindent\textbf{RQ4: Does \morph{} preserve coherent tissue structure?}\quad
\label{sec:rq4}
Gene-wise PCC does not test whether predictions preserve coherent tissue regions. We cluster spots
using either predicted or measured expression and compare the induced domains with adjusted Rand

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{RQ5: Is \morph{} computationally efficient?}\quad
\label{sec:rq5}
\morph{} contains $4.88$M parameters and predicts a complete slide in one forward pass
(Table~\ref{tab:complexity}). Its advantage over the baselines is not minimum parameter count ---
ST-Net and BLEEP are smaller --- but the combination of multi-scale spatial context and direct
inference. Model-only latency grows from $3.5$\,ms for 500 spots to $64.7$\,ms for 10,000 spots on

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
Three findings merit emphasis. First, a compact direct predictor is competitive when features,
gene panels, splits, and model selection are controlled; the $4.9\%$ relative gain over the
strongest baseline rests on matched experimental conditions, not a larger model. Second, pooled
multi-organ performance is not a substitute for unseen-organ evaluation: the drop from $0.760$
(pooled) to $0.490$ (LOOO) shows that anatomical shift remains a separate and harder problem. Third,
local distance-biased attention is the dominant contributor in the factorial study; global and slide
pathways provide moderate complementary gains, and the complete model ($\mathrm{C111}$) achieves
the best mean across both tested cohorts.

Direct and generative models serve different purposes. STEM \cite{zhu2025diffusion} models a
conditional expression distribution and can represent one-to-many uncertainty through iterative
sampling. \morph{} produces a point estimate optimised for correlation and squared error. Its
benefits --- deterministic, single-pass inference and explicit coordinate invariance --- do not
imply that morphology uniquely determines expression.

\textbf{Limitations.} The evaluation predicts $50$ genes from frozen UNI+CONCH embeddings; results
may not extend to transcriptome-scale output or end-to-end image encoding. LOOO uses a fixed
benchmark panel and does not test platform or institution shift simultaneously. Coordinate invariance
holds only for the spatial pathway with fixed image features; rotating the histology image can change
its frozen embedding. Domain-recovery analysis covers only two baselines and has high slide-level
variance. Pooled folds are sample-disjoint but cannot be verified as patient-disjoint where patient
identifiers are unavailable.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
We developed \morph{}, a coordinate-invariant local--global spatial transformer for predicting gene
expression from histology. Pairwise-distance encoding makes the spatial pathway invariant to rigid
coordinate transformations by construction, while three parallel context signals --- local $k$NN
attention, global self-attention, and slide-level pooling --- together produce complete-slide
predictions in a single pass. These design choices directly address our design goals that
multi-scale context is necessary and that architectural positional bias should not break under
coordinate shift. Under matched HEST-1k evaluation, \morph{} improves mean intra-cohort correlation
and remains competitive in LOOO transfer; the pooled-to-LOOO gap exposes the remaining difficulty of
anatomical transfer as the central open challenge.

For future work, it would be valuable to explore how conditioning on slide-level metadata (organ
type, sequencing platform) can narrow this anatomical gap --- for instance, through FiLM or
adaptive layer normalisation conditioners that are available at inference time without requiring
expression labels. Scaling the gene panel beyond 50 genes, incorporating end-to-end image encoding,
and combining \morph{}'s geometric structure with calibrated generative uncertainty modelling
(as in STEM) are further natural directions.

\section{Proofs of the formal guarantees}
\label{app:formal_guarantees}

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:coordinate_invariance}}
For every pair of spots $i,j$, orthogonality of $R$ gives
\begin{align}
\left\|R\mathbf{p}_i+\mathbf{t}-(R\mathbf{p}_j+\mathbf{t})\right\|_2
&=\left\|R(\mathbf{p}_i-\mathbf{p}_j)\right\|_2 \\
&=\left\|\mathbf{p}_i-\mathbf{p}_j\right\|_2.
\end{align}
Hence, transforming the coordinate frame leaves the complete distance matrix unchanged. It also
leaves the $k$NN graph, radial basis features, and distance-dependent attention biases unchanged.
The input projection is unchanged because $\mathbf{F}$ is fixed. At every layer, the local pathway
therefore receives the same features, neighbours, and distance biases, while the global and slide
pathways do not use coordinates. Their outputs, the residual fusion, and the feed-forward update are
identical. Induction over the $L$ layers gives identical final representations and predictions,
proving Proposition~\ref{prop:coordinate_invariance}.

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:permutation_equivariance}}
Let $\mathbf{F}'=Q\mathbf{F}$ and $\mathbf{P}'=Q\mathbf{P}$. The pairwise distance matrix is relabelled
as $\mathbf{D}'=Q\mathbf{D}Q^\top$. Under equivariant tie handling, the $k$NN graph and its radial
basis edge features are relabelled by the same permutation. The input projection and all pointwise
linear maps, layer normalizations, activations, and feed-forward networks commute with $Q$.

For local attention, permuting the spots only relabels each neighbourhood and its attention sum, so
the local output is multiplied by $Q$. Global self-attention is likewise permutation equivariant
because its query-key score matrix is transformed by $Q$ on both axes and its value matrix by $Q$.
The slide attention scores are permuted, but their weighted sum is unchanged; broadcasting that same
summary to all spots is equivariant. Consequently, residual fusion preserves equivariance at every
layer. Induction over the layers and application of the pointwise prediction head yield
$f_\theta(Q\mathbf{F},Q\mathbf{P})=Qf_\theta(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{P})$, proving
Proposition~\ref{prop:permutation_equivariance}.
